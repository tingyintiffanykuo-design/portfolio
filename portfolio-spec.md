## 1. Goal

Update the owner's portfolio so it gets them interviews for mid-level UX roles, while honestly representing what they did.

Specific goals:
1. Position them as a **designer whose edge is research and strategy**, not as a researcher who also dabbles in design.
2. Use AI as a sparring partner and production assistant for the portfolio work, without letting it invent facts, metrics, or outcomes.
3. Remove any gap between what the portfolio claims and what the owner can defend in an interview.

## 4. Market analysis (11 job descriptions)

Source: a document of 11 JDs the owner collected (7 design roles, 4 research roles). Roles: Amazon HVH UX Designer I; Amazon Delivery Experience UX Designer (3+ yrs); Microsoft UX Researcher (Windows and Devices); REI Product Researcher (3+); Noosphere UX Researcher (5+, in-person); Amazon Research Strategist (3+); Google Chrome Disco UX Designer (4+); Amazon Robotics Senior Product Designer (7+); Amazon Fulfillment (AFT) Senior UX Designer (6+); Product Designer, Agent Experience (3-5, regulated/insurance-style product); Ellevation Education Product Designer (5+).

### Patterns
1. **AI appears in 9 of 11 JDs**, in two forms: using AI in your own workflow (AI-assisted prototyping, synthesis, working in an IDE with Claude Code or Cursor), and **designing for AI products** (legibility, confidence, override, edge and failure states; the Amazon HVH "trust layer" language is the clearest example). The second form is rarer and fits the owner's strengths.
2. **Research must end in a decision.** Nearly every JD asks for synthesis into actionable recommendations.
3. **A point of view is expected**: presenting options with trade-offs, advocating with evidence.
4. **Complex, high-stakes systems**: operations, robotics, regulated products. "Systems thinking, not just screens."
5. **Close partnership with PM and engineering**; some want coded prototypes.
6. **Measurable outcomes** ("outcomes over output").
7. **Field and real-world grounding.**
8. **Responsible practice**: accessibility, privacy, trust, compliance.

### Fit by years of experience
- Good fit: Amazon HVH (UX Designer I), Amazon Delivery (3+), Agent Experience (3-5), Microsoft UXR (2+), REI and Research Strategist (3+ in research).
- Stretch: Chrome (4), Ellevation (5+), Noosphere (5+).
- Senior, not a near-term target: Amazon Fulfillment (6+), Amazon Robotics (7+).

### Gaps identified
- Coded or AI-assisted prototyping (increasingly expected, even for design roles).
- Visual craft: **Chrome Disco asks for typography, branding, and visual storytelling and is a poor fit.**
- Design system contributions.
- Measurable impact.

## 5. Positioning decisions and rationale

### 5.1 Lead as a designer; research is the edge (decided)
**Why:** Designer roles screen for design craft first, so strong research becomes a differentiator. Researcher roles screen for research depth first (study plans, methods, stats, often 5+ years), and design skill barely enters the evaluation. The same profile is therefore worth more in the designer pool. Researcher roles would require building a separate set of research artifacts. If the owner later wants one, they can tailor the resume and add research artifacts then.

"Hybrid" is also read as hedging by hiring managers, who screen for a specific role.

### 5.2 "Research shows up as how I work, not who I am" (decided; the owner explicitly asked to keep this throughout)
In practice:
- Case studies open with the problem and the design; research appears as the reason behind decisions.
- Research artifacts (synthesis, journey maps) are shown as steps toward a design decision, not standalone deliverables.
- Headline and bio describe what the owner designs; research is the method.
- Each case study keeps roughly **40% research / 60% design**.
- Do not title the owner "UX Designer & Researcher."

### 5.3 How to avoid looking like "all research, little design" (decided)
- Show design thinking, not just polish: 2-3 directions explored and why one was chosen, empty/error/edge states, final flows or a prototype.
- For dashboards and monitoring tools, judge on information architecture, interaction logic, and states, not visual flair.
- Keep visuals clean and consistent by building on an established component library; restraint reads as craft. Have a designer friend review the screens.

### 5.4 AI claims policy (decided)
- **Do not claim AI-powered product experience.** Interviewers will probe it.
- Show AI in the workflow specifically: which tool, which task, what the owner verified. Example of the right level of detail: used AI to cluster interview transcripts, then re-checked every theme against raw quotes.
- Keep "AI-powered" out of the headline until a concept project exists. Then it can be a subtitle line or an added case study.

### 5.5 Headline and subtitle (partly decided)
- **Headline (liked; effectively chosen):** "I design clarity for complex operational tools."
- Subtitle must **not start with "I design"** (owner request). Keep the phrase **"rely on"** (liked). Tone: grounded, specific, not too bold, not too broad.
- Subtitle candidates offered (no final pick yet):
  1. "Software that robot engineers, lab teams, and security analysts rely on, shaped by how they actually work." (recommended)
  2. "Control interfaces, lab systems, and monitoring platforms that experts rely on every day."
  3. "For the people who monitor, control, and troubleshoot complex systems, from fulfillment robots to security platforms."
- The unifying thread across the projects: **tools for experts who monitor and control complex systems under pressure**, which matches Amazon Robotics, Fulfillment, and HVH-style roles.

## 7. Portfolio-wide requirements

1. Never invent metrics, outcomes, or roles. Qualitative impact is acceptable and more honest.
2. Credit collaborators by name in role lines and in the relevant sections. State what the owner owned vs. what the team owned.
3. Keep the owner's role line leading with design.
4. Lead each case study with an "at a glance" summary (problem, what I did, key insight, status/outcome).
5. Show hard design moments: alternatives considered and why one was chosen.
6. Be explicit about evidence limits (test participants, no post-launch data, simulated data) and what would be done next.
7. Handle confidentiality: get permission, redact properly or redraw, and say in interviews that artifacts were redrawn. Never fake screens or research artifacts with image generators.
8. Label self-initiated work as self-initiated.
9. Use AI for structure, editing, critique, and diagramming; the core writing must be in the owner's own words.
10. Test the finished site with AI as a skeptical hiring manager, then with 2-3 humans (ideally one designer and one non-designer), including a 5-second memory test.

## 8. Device Management (DMS) case study

Page: https://tiffanytykuo.webflow.io/work/benq-dms. I could read only the **text** of the page, not the images or GIFs, so visual layout and polish are **not reviewed**.

### 8.1 Page facts (as published)
- Timeline: 1 year (2022-2023). Feature team: 2 UX designers, 2 UI designers, 1 PM, 13 engineers.
- Owner's published role: UX Designer, "User research, Feature planning, Interface and Interaction design, Project & Product management, and Design Ops."
- Context: K-12 districts in the US manage large numbers of BenQ digital whiteboards through a cloud DMS; districts resemble large corporations in scale.
- Research: contextual inquiries at 6 Texas schools (all existing clients; owner did not have full control over participant selection). Three problems found: (1) batch configuration is complex, (2) hard to see overall status of many devices, (3) hard to track and resolve issues (operation logs unreadable, help channels unclear).
- Define: JTBD (reduce chances of issues; make troubleshooting efficient) and three principles: bundle settings, present data readably, make operations traceable.
- Develop/Deliver: sitemap, lo-fi wireframes (blurred for NDA), three delivered changes shown in GIFs: Policy, grouped donut-chart cards, clear cues to track device status and log.
- Test: remote Maze sessions with internal FAEs.
- Beyond: built the company's first web design system, led the first design critique, set up team collaboration norms in Figma, created a design backlog list.
- Reflections: don't let big-client requests dilute the product; talk to engineers early.

### 8.2 What works (keep)
- Real field research with 6 schools.
- Three design decisions showing judgment (see section 9).
- Candor about constraints (internal testers, NDA, selected participants).
- Design system and critique work (JDs repeatedly ask for pattern and design-system contribution).

### 8.4 Test section restructure
Why: the current section asks readers to trust findings without letting them judge the evidence (no participant count, no tasks listed). The owner's tasks are good and specific and should be shown.

Facts confirmed by the owner:
- Test focused on usability of the new **Policy** feature, with internal FAEs (Field Application Engineers) with school device-management experience, remote via Maze.
- Three tasks: (1) deploy a policy for the first time; (2) deploy again after adding new devices; (3) adjust settings within the policy and deploy to a group of devices.
- Three findings: FAEs preferred Policy over the old approach (fewer omissions and errors, easy to learn); usage depends on whether settings need constant updates and whether they are consistent district-wide; participants did not understand whether a later policy would **override** or **add to** an earlier one.
- Design change: settings were split into two types (admin-only, mostly unchanged; and periodically updated, adjustable by classroom needs), and the relationship between devices and the two types was clarified.

Proposed structure: Goal; Method (N participants, who they were, remote on Maze, 3 tasks); Findings per task (table: Task / What we observed / What changed); Design change; Limits (why internal FAEs were acceptable and how they may bias results, e.g. familiarity making them more forgiving than real district IT admins).

**Role:** UX Designer: information architecture, interaction design, lo-fi wireframes, and field research. Visual design by two UI designers.

**At a glance:**
- **Problem:** K-12 districts manage large numbers of interactive whiteboards with a DMS built for one-device-at-a-time settings.
- **What I did:** (unconfirmed: scoped the project and) planned the information architecture, took part in contextual inquiries at 6 Texas schools, designed the lo-fi wireframes and interaction logic that the UI designers turned into final screens, and tested the new Policy feature with (N unconfirmed) FAEs.

## 9. DMS product design decisions (as documented) and why

| Decision | Why | Rejected alternative |
|---|---|---|
| **Policy**: bundle settings, apply like attaching a label, edit once to reconfigure many devices; "shopping cart" metaphor (add only the settings you need) | Admins prefer consistent district-wide settings with small exceptions; the old design forced re-selecting target devices every time | Showing every setting on the policy editing page. Rejected because some settings are adjusted by end users (teachers) and IT does not want to override them, and deploying many settings to many devices is resource-intensive |
| Dashboard shows **count of devices per status** with a "See More" button to load on demand, plus a dedicated page storing all events | Overall progress percentage is hard to compute across devices (chip performance and network quality vary) and more events overload backend processing | A real-time overall progress percentage |
| **Group similar charts in a card; donut charts** instead of bar graphs; keep access to individual device detail | Better suited to large amounts of data; the old per-device dashboard caused overload and almost nobody used it | Per-device presentation and bar graphs |
| **Embed cues in the flow** to track device status and log | Make troubleshooting timely and traceable | The hard-to-read operation log and unclear help channels |
| **Split settings into two types** after testing (admin-only/mostly unchanged vs. periodically updated/classroom-driven) and clarify how devices relate to each | FAEs could not tell whether a second policy overrides or adds to the first | A single undifferentiated policy mechanism |

Still missing (to ask the owner): who proposed the shopping-cart idea, who pushed back, and what other approaches were considered.
